Bringing Money to Switzerland: What Expats Need to Know

Switzerland does not tax the transfer itself. But what you declare, how you transfer, and what the bank asks for are questions worth understanding before you move.

Updated August 2026
4 min read

Key Takeaways

01Switzerland does not tax the transfer of capital — bringing savings from abroad triggers no Swiss tax on the amount transferred
02Capital accumulated before Swiss residency enters your wealth declaration — subject to wealth tax (0.1%–1%), not income tax
03Future income generated by foreign assets — interest, dividends, rent — IS taxable in Switzerland from the moment you become resident
04Swiss banks must verify the origin of significant transfers — prepare documentation in advance to avoid delays
05Switzerland shares account data with most countries under CRS — undeclared foreign capital is increasingly difficult to conceal
06Bringing more than CHF 10,000 in cash into Switzerland requires a customs declaration — no tax, but mandatory

The Good News — Switzerland Does Not Tax the Transfer

✅ No capital transfer tax

Switzerland has no tax on the transfer of capital. You can bring savings from abroad to a Swiss account without paying any tax on the amount transferred. What Switzerland taxes is income and wealth — not the origin of money or the fact that you transferred it. Capital accumulated before Swiss residency is not taxed as Swiss income — it is declared as wealth and subject to wealth tax (0.1%–1% per year), not income tax at 30%–40%.

What You Must Declare

From the moment you become a Swiss tax resident, your worldwide assets must be declared in your first tax return. The distinction between what is taxed as income vs what is taxed as wealth is critical.

🏦 Wealth declaration — 0.1% to 1%/year

  • Foreign bank account balances (31 December)
  • Foreign investment portfolios (31 December)
  • Property abroad — estimated market value
  • Other significant foreign assets
  • Pillar 3a balances

📈 Taxable as income — 25% to 43%

  • Interest received on foreign accounts
  • Dividends from foreign investments
  • Rental income from property abroad
  • Any other income generated by foreign assets

⚠ The transfer itself is not taxable — the future income is. Bring CHF 500,000 in savings to Switzerland: no tax on the transfer. But if those CHF 500,000 generate CHF 15,000/year in dividends, those dividends are taxable Swiss income from the first year of residency.

How to Transfer — Three Options

🏦

SWIFT Bank Transfer

Most common and secure. Bank in country of origin sends SWIFT transfer to Swiss account. For large amounts, the Swiss bank may request documentation on the origin of funds.

Best for: any amount, especially above CHF 50,000

📱

Wise / Revolut

Significantly better exchange rates than traditional banks. For CHF 10,000 or less, savings of CHF 100–300 vs a bank transfer. Fast and reliable for smaller amounts.

Best for: amounts below CHF 50,000

💵

Physical Cash

Legal to bring cash into Switzerland. Above CHF 10,000 (or equivalent), must be declared at Swiss customs. No tax — mandatory declaration only. Omitting can result in confiscation and fines.

Best for: small amounts only

What the Bank Will Ask — KYC/AML

Swiss banks are legally required to verify the origin of significant funds. For transfers above CHF 10,000–100,000 (threshold varies by bank), expect to provide:

Documents typically requested

Bank statements from country of origin showing how the capital accumulated over time
Tax declarations from country of origin showing the capital was declared
Inheritance documentation, property sale contract, or other specific origin evidence
Employment contracts or other documents explaining the source of income

✅ This is not personal — it is legally mandatory for everyone. KYC/AML checks apply to all customers regardless of nationality or background. Prepare documentation in advance and the process is straightforward. Banks that cannot verify the origin of funds are legally required to decline the transfer.

The Key Principle

"Switzerland does not tax where your money came from. It taxes what your money earns once you are here."

Undeclared Capital in Your Country of Origin

Switzerland does not automatically inform your country of origin about your accounts here — but the situation is increasingly complex due to the Common Reporting Standard (CRS). Under CRS, Switzerland automatically shares information about accounts held by non-residents with the account holder's country of residence. This covers most European countries, Brazil, and many others.

⚠ If you have capital not declared in your country of origin, consult a tax lawyer in that country before bringing it to Switzerland. Voluntary regularisation is almost always significantly less costly than a tax investigation. Switzerland's automatic exchange agreements make the "invisible account" strategy increasingly unrealistic.

For US Citizens — Additional Rules

🇺🇸 US citizen obligations when transferring to Switzerland

  • Bringing money to Switzerland does not create additional US tax — capital already taxed in the US remains taxed
  • FBAR required if Swiss account balance exceeds $10,000 at any point during the year (FinCEN Form 114)
  • FATCA Form 8938 required if total foreign financial assets exceed $200,000 at year-end
  • Swiss banks already report account data to the IRS under the FATCA agreement — non-disclosure is not viable

Before You Ask

I have CHF 200,000 in savings abroad. Do I pay tax when I bring it to Switzerland?▾
No. The transfer itself is not taxed. The CHF 200,000 enters your wealth declaration and is subject to wealth tax (approximately 0.1%–1%/year on the net value). But any income generated by that capital in Switzerland — interest, dividends — is taxable as income from the first year of residency.
Can the Swiss bank refuse my transfer?▾
The bank can request additional documentation, delay processing, or in extreme cases decline. For legal, properly documented capital, the transfer processes without problems. Prepare origin-of-funds documents in advance — especially for amounts above CHF 50,000.
I have foreign investments I never declared in my home country. What should I do?▾
Consult a tax lawyer in your country of origin before taking any action. Most countries have voluntary disclosure programmes that significantly reduce penalties compared to a formal investigation. Do not transfer undeclared capital to Switzerland hoping it goes unnoticed — CRS data sharing has made this increasingly impossible.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
Before transferring significant amounts — especially if the capital has a complex origin (inheritance, business sale, property) or if you have questions about how to declare it correctly in Switzerland. An advisor can guide the documentation process and ensure the first Swiss tax return is filed correctly.
