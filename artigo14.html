How Stock Options and RSUs Are Taxed in Switzerland

Quick Answer: Stock options and RSUs are among the most valuable — and least understood — parts of a Swiss pay package.

The good news: once your shares vest and are taxed as employment income, any further growth is tax-free when you sell. Switzerland has no capital gains tax on private wealth.

The challenge: the tax bill at vesting or exercise can be large, the rules differ by type of equity, and if you moved to Switzerland partway through the vesting period, the calculation becomes considerably more complex.

Key Takeaways

  • RSUs are taxed when they vest; stock options are generally taxed when you exercise them
  • If you moved to Switzerland mid-vesting, only the Swiss share of the vesting period is taxable here
  • B Permit holders: your employer withholds tax at source on RSUs and options when the tax event happens
  • AHV social security also applies to equity income — 5.3% with no cap, on top of income tax, which many people forget to budget for
The basics

What Are RSUs and Stock Options?

They are two ways a company pays part of your salary in its own shares instead of cash. Both are common at multinationals and tech companies.

RSU — shares you receive later

A restricted stock unit is a promise: "you will receive these shares, as long as you still work here." You pay nothing for them.

Each day a batch becomes yours is a vesting date. Leave the company early and you lose the batches not yet vested.

Stock option — the right to buy cheap

The right to buy company shares at a price fixed in advance — the exercise price. Using that right is called exercising.

You buy at the fixed price shares now worth more. The difference is your gain.

In short: with an RSU you receive the shares; with a stock option you get the right to buy them cheaply. For Switzerland, both count as salary.

RSUs

Taxed When They Vest

On the day your RSUs vest, the market value of the shares is treated as employment income and taxed accordingly — added to your salary for that period.

Example: you receive 500 RSUs from your US employer. They vest on 1 January 2026, when the share price is CHF 80. The value of CHF 40,000 is added to your 2026 taxable income.

Shares blocked from sale? If your plan keeps the shares locked for a period after vesting, the taxable value is reduced by about 6% for each year they stay blocked (for up to ten years).

Stock options

Taxed When You Exercise

The taxable amount is the difference between the market price on the day you exercise and the exercise price you pay.

Example: you hold options on 1,000 shares with an exercise price of CHF 30. You exercise when the market price is CHF 75. The gain of CHF 45,000 is taxable employment income.

The exception: options that are listed on a stock exchange and freely tradable are taxed when you receive them, not when you exercise. Most employee plans use blocked or unlisted options, so the exercise rule is the one most people meet.

After vesting

What Happens Next?

Switzerland does not tax capital gains on the sale of shares held as private wealth — as long as you are not classed as a professional securities trader. If your shares double in value after vesting, that gain is entirely yours. It is one of the most attractive features of Switzerland for equity holders.

Two things do continue: the shares count towards your wealth tax each year, and any dividends are taxed as income.

Hidden cost

AHV on Equity Income

This is the part most expats don't see coming. AHV — Swiss social security — applies to equity income at the same rate as salary: 5.3% deducted from you, with another 5.3% paid by your employer. Unlike many countries, Switzerland has no ceiling on AHV.

In practice, a single CHF 200,000 RSU vesting adds about CHF 10,600 in AHV on top of income tax.

Spreading equity events over several years, where your plan allows it, can lower your income tax, because the rate rises with income. AHV is a flat rate with no cap — spreading does not reduce it.

Moved mid-vesting

Only the Swiss Share Is Taxed Here

This is where equity taxation gets genuinely complex — and where most mistakes happen. When your tax residence moves to Switzerland during the vesting period, Switzerland generally taxes the award pro rata temporis — in proportion to time: the number of days you worked in Switzerland, divided by all working days in the vesting period. Switzerland follows the OECD Model Commentary for this calculation.

Example: you receive RSUs in 2022, vesting over three years in January 2025. You work in London in 2022, split 2023 equally between London and Geneva, and work only in Geneva in 2024. The vesting period covers about 780 working days, of which about 390 were in Switzerland.

US citizens

An Extra Layer of Complexity

If you are a US citizen receiving equity in Switzerland, you have obligations in both countries at once. RSUs, stock options and ISOs (a US type of option with special US tax treatment) must be split between US and foreign income based on where you worked, and the Swiss and US tax events may not fall in the same year.

The FEIE — which lets Americans abroad exclude up to $132,900 of foreign salary from US tax in 2026 — is usually used up by your salary. For equity, the Foreign Tax Credit, which subtracts the Swiss tax you already paid from your US tax bill, does most of the work. Our article on US citizens in Switzerland explains both.

Getting this wrong in either direction — overpaying or underpaying — is one of the most common mistakes among US expats with equity compensation.

Common Mistakes

01

Assuming your employer handles everything

Withholding is not always calculated correctly, particularly in international situations. And if your gross income goes above CHF 120,000 in a year — which a large vesting can cause — a B Permit holder must file a full tax return for that year. It is worth reviewing the calculation independently.

02

Not tracking work days between countries

The pro rata calculation needs accurate records of the days you worked in each country during the vesting period. Many people don't keep them and only discover the problem at filing time.

03

Selling right after vesting without a plan

Selling immediately is not necessarily wrong — but doing it without understanding the tax consequences and timing options means missing opportunities that are hard to recover later.

Before You Ask

What if I have a C Permit?▾
There is no withholding at source: tax on your equity income is settled through your annual tax return. It is still worth checking that the amount your employer reports on your salary certificate is correct.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
Ideally before a large vesting event — not after. There is room to plan around timing, around voluntary pension fund buy-ins (which are deductible and limited only by your personal buy-in gap, so they can offset a large vesting year) and, for Americans, around coordination with US obligations.

Equity compensation is one of the areas where Swiss taxation most rewards those who plan ahead — and most penalises those who don't.
