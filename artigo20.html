Financing Property in Switzerland as an Expat

Key Takeaways

  • You need a 20% down payment — at least half of it (10% of the price) from your own savings, not your pension fund
  • Swiss mortgages come in two tiers — the first (up to 65%) has no repayment deadline: you pay only interest, and the debt can stay until you sell
  • The rule that matters most: your yearly housing costs can't exceed one third (33%) of your gross income
  • Until 2028, homeowners pay income tax on a rent they never receive — the imputed rental value. The Swiss keep their mortgage because the interest offsets it
  • From 2029 the imputed rental value disappears, but mortgage interest stops being deductible — "never pay it off" no longer makes sense for many owners

The Minimum Down Payment

Swiss banks require at least 20% of the property price in your own money. Of that 20%, at least 10% must be personal savings — the bank won't accept the whole amount coming from Pillar 2 (your workplace pension fund). The other 10% can come from Pillar 2 or Pillar 3a (your private retirement savings account).

Not living in Switzerland yet? Banks ask non-residents for 35% to 50% down — and the Lex Koller law restricts property purchases by people living abroad. In Geneva, buying without Swiss residence is very difficult.

The Two-Tier Mortgage

Switzerland splits the bank loan into two mortgages on the same property, with very different rules:

1st mortgage · up to 65%

No deadline to repay

You pay only the interest. The debt can stay until you sell — the sale proceeds then repay the bank first. You can pay it off whenever you like, but you don't have to.

The bank may ask you to reduce it in a few cases: at retirement, if your pension no longer passes the one-third rule; at the end of a fixed-rate term, if you don't renew; or if the property loses a lot of value.

Debt stays the same — interest only
2nd mortgage · 65% to 80%

Works like a normal loan

It must be paid back within 15 years, or by retirement age — whichever comes first. This repayment of the loan itself (not the interest) is called amortisation.

Repaid over 15 years

The first tier is the debt Swiss residents keep for decades — because of the imputed rental value, explained below.

Fixed Rate or SARON

Fixed rate (Festhypothek)

1.8–2.4%

For 10 years in 2026. The rate is locked for the whole term (2 to 15 years), so you're protected if rates rise. The most popular choice among expats who want predictable payments.

SARON (variable)

0.9–1.3%

Follows SARON, the Swiss overnight interest rate between banks. Cheaper today, but it rises when the market does. Many buyers split: part fixed, part SARON.

The One-Third Rule: How Much House You Can Afford

In plain terms: your yearly housing costs — interest, repayment of the 2nd mortgage and maintenance — can't be more than one third (33%) of your gross salary. This is the rule that decides whether the bank lends you the money.

Watch out: for this calculation the bank doesn't use today's interest rate — the one it will actually charge you — but a fictitious safety rate of 5%. It does the maths on that rate to make sure you could still pay if rates went up. That's why the limit is tighter than your real payments would suggest.

At CHF 800,000, that's CHF 48,000 a year × 3 = CHF 144,000 of gross income the bank will require. Buying as a couple? Both salaries count together — CHF 80,000 + CHF 64,000 = CHF 144,000 works too.

The Imputed Rental Value — Why the Swiss Keep Their Mortgage (Until 2028)

In plain terms: today, if you live in a home you own, you pay tax on a fictitious rent — the imputed rental value (valeur locative in Geneva, Eigenmietwert in German). The tax office adds to your income the rent you would get if you let the property out, even though you live in it. It's usually 60–70% of the market rent.

In return, you can deduct your mortgage interest and maintenance costs. This is why the Swiss never pay off their mortgage: the interest offsets the fictitious rent. Pay the mortgage off completely and the fictitious rent hits your tax bill in full, with nothing to balance it — with low rates, you can even pay more tax than if you didn't own a home.

Illustrative figures: imputed rental value 60–70% of a ≈ CHF 30,000 market rent, interest at ≈ 1.8%, marginal tax rate ≈ 30% (upper-middle income in Geneva). Maintenance deduction not included. The real imputed value is set by the Geneva tax office for each property.

In other words: on a CHF 800,000 home that's fully paid off, the tax office adds a fictitious rent of CHF 18,000–21,000 a year (CHF 1,500–1,750 a month) to your salary — and taxes it: around CHF 5,400–6,300 a year, or CHF 450–525 a month. In practice, you pay the tax office a kind of rent every month to live in a home you own. This ends in 2029, when the imputed rental value is abolished.

And the more you pay off, the worse it gets. The fictitious rent stays the same every year — but as the debt shrinks, so does the interest, leaving less and less to deduct. The more the home is yours, the more tax you pay on it.

What Changes in 2029

From 1 January 2029, the imputed rental value is abolished for the home you live in — approved by popular vote in September 2025, with the date set by the Federal Council. At the same time, mortgage interest and maintenance costs on that home stop being deductible.

Until 2028

✕ Tax on the fictitious rent
✓ Mortgage interest deductible
✓ Maintenance deductible

From 2029

✓ No tax on the fictitious rent
✕ Interest no longer deductible
✕ Maintenance no longer deductible

Exception: first-time buyers who live in the home keep a limited interest deduction for ten years.

With the fictitious rent and the interest deduction disappearing together, the main reason never to pay off the mortgage disappears too. The mortgage becomes just a cost, and the decision gets simple: if your money earns more elsewhere than the interest you'd pay, keep the mortgage; if it's sitting in an account earning nothing, paying it off makes sense. If you buy before 2029, run the numbers for both periods — until 2028 and from 2029.

What about wealth tax? It barely affects this decision: paying off the mortgage with your savings doesn't change it, because what you own falls by exactly as much as what you owe — your net wealth stays the same. And the wealth tax rules don't change with the reform.

Common Mistakes

1

Deciding to pay off or keep the mortgage without looking at 2029

Until 2028, paying off the whole mortgage usually raises your tax because of the imputed rental value. From 2029, keeping a large mortgage can be expensive because the interest is no longer deductible. The right choice depends on the year and on where your money is.

2

Using all your Pillar 2 for the down payment

Withdrawing Pillar 2 reduces your future pension and is taxed when you take the money out. Use only what you need.

3

Not comparing banks

Rates vary from bank to bank. A difference of 0.3% on a CHF 640,000 mortgage is almost CHF 2,000 a year.

Before You Ask

Can I use Pillar 2 for the down payment?▾
Yes — for up to 10% of the property price, as long as at least another 10% comes from your own savings. The withdrawal is taxed, and you have to pay the money back into Pillar 2 if you sell the property.
Should I choose a fixed rate or SARON?▾
It depends on how much risk you're comfortable with. SARON is cheaper now but can rise; a fixed rate gives you certainty. Many expats split the mortgage — part fixed for safety, part SARON to benefit from today's low rates.
Do I need an advisor to buy a home?▾

In most cases, no. Banks have mortgage specialists who explain the whole process free of charge — the one-third rule, the two tiers, fixed vs SARON and the documents you need. It's worth talking to two or three banks.

An independent advisor makes sense when your questions go beyond the loan: if you'll use Pillar 2 or 3a for the down payment, if your income is irregular or comes from several countries, or if you want to understand the tax impact of buying before or after 2029.

Financing a home in Switzerland rewards those who plan ahead. For the loan itself, the bank's specialist is usually enough. When Pillar 2, income from several countries or the 2029 imputed rental value reform come into play, an independent advisor helps you get the numbers right before you sign.
