What Makes Swiss Financing Different

The Swiss mortgage system has its own rules that surprise almost every expat. There is a mandatory stress test at 5% even when current rates are below 2%, a two-tier mortgage structure with different repayment obligations, and a tax strategy that leads Swiss residents to deliberately never pay off their mortgage. Understanding these three points changes how you approach the entire purchase.

The Minimum Down Payment

Swiss banks require a minimum of 20% of the property value in equity. Of that 20%, at least 10% must be personal savings — the bank will not accept the full amount from Pillar 2. The remaining 10% can come from Pillar 2 (employer pension fund) or Pillar 3a. For non-residents without a Swiss residence permit, the minimum rises to 35% to 50%.

The Two-Tier Mortgage System

Switzerland uses a unique system of two mortgages simultaneously on the same property:

1st Mortgage — up to 65%

No repayment required

The bank does not require you to pay this down. This is where Swiss residents keep debt active indefinitely — it is the wealth tax shield.

2nd Mortgage — 65% to 80%

Must be repaid in 15 years

This portion must be amortised within 15 years or by retirement age, whichever comes first.

Fixed Rate vs SARON

Fixed Rate (Festhypothek)

1.8–2.4%

10-year fixed in 2026. Protects against rate rises. Terms from 2 to 15 years. Most popular choice for expats who want predictability.

SARON (Variable)

0.9–1.3%

Linked to the Swiss overnight rate. Cheaper now but rises with the market. Many buyers split — part fixed, part SARON.

The 5% Stress Test — Why Many Expats Are Rejected

Even if the current rate is 1.5%, the bank calculates your repayment capacity at 5%. Total housing costs — calculated at 5% — cannot exceed 33% of gross annual income.

The Mortgage as a Tax Shield — Why Swiss Residents Never Pay It Off

The wealth tax is calculated on net wealth — the property value minus the outstanding mortgage. A large mortgage dramatically reduces the tax owed. And mortgage interest is deductible from taxable income on top of that. Two tax benefits simultaneously.

Wealth tax per year: ~CHF 2,500–3,500

The Swiss Strategy

"Swiss residents deliberately never pay off the first mortgage. The debt is not a burden — it is a tax shield. And the interest is deductible from taxable income on top of that."

The Imputed Rental Value — and Its Abolition in 2029

Currently Switzerland taxes a fictitious income called the imputed rental value — the amount you would receive if you rented out your property. This is added to your taxable income even though you live there yourself. In exchange, mortgage interest and maintenance costs are deductible.

✅ Big change coming: The Swiss Parliament has approved the abolition of the imputed rental value from 1 January 2029. With the abolition, mortgage interest will also no longer be deductible for primary residences. Anyone buying before 2029 should model how this affects their tax situation from 2029 onwards.

Common Mistakes

❌
Paying off the mortgage
The biggest fiscal error a Swiss property owner can make. The first mortgage is a powerful wealth tax shield. Keeping it active indefinitely is strategy, not debt.
❌
Using all of Pillar 2 for the down payment
Drawing down Pillar 2 reduces your future pension and triggers tax at the moment of withdrawal. Use only what is necessary.
❌
Not comparing banks
Rates vary significantly. Some cantonal banks and Raiffeisen use a 4.5% stress test instead of 5% — this can make the difference between approval and rejection.
❌
Ignoring the 2029 imputed rental value abolition
Anyone buying before 2029 should model how the elimination of this deduction affects their tax situation from 2029 onwards — it changes the mortgage interest equation.

Before You Ask

Can I use Pillar 2 for the down payment?▾
Yes. You can use up to 10% of the property value from Pillar 2. But at least another 10% must be personal savings. There is a tax on the withdrawal and you must return the funds to Pillar 2 if you sell the property.
Should I choose a fixed rate or SARON?▾
It depends on your profile. SARON is cheaper now but can rise. A fixed rate gives predictability. Many expats split the mortgage — part fixed for security, part SARON to benefit from current low rates.
How much income do I need to buy a CHF 800,000 property?▾
Approximately CHF 140,000 gross annually — calculated by the 5% stress test. With CHF 160,000 down payment, the mortgage is CHF 640,000. At 5%: CHF 32,000 interest + amortisation + maintenance = ~CHF 46,000. Divided by 33% = ~CHF 140,000 minimum gross income.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
Before making any offer. The stress test, mortgage structure, Pillar 2 use, and the wealth tax strategy all depend very much on your specific situation and income.

Swiss mortgage financing rewards those who plan ahead. The combination of the stress test, the two-tier structure, the wealth tax strategy and the 2029 imputed rental value reform means that professional advice typically pays for itself many times over.
