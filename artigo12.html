Pillar 3a: Bank, Insurance, or ETF Portfolio — Which Is Right for You?

Quick Answer: Most expats open their Pillar 3a at the same bank where their salary is paid — and never revisit the decision.

But every bank, insurer and app has its own 3a product, with its own rules: where the money is invested, what you pay in fees and how flexible it is. Those differences, added up over the years, are what change the final amount.

That default choice can cost tens of thousands of francs over a career in Switzerland. Understanding the three options before choosing is one of the most valuable financial decisions a new arrival can make.

Key Takeaways

  • Three types of Pillar 3a exist — bank savings account, insurance product, ETF portfolio — and all three give the same tax deduction
  • The best-established low-cost digital providers in 2026: finpension (0.39%), VIAC (up to about 0.40%) and frankly (0.43%)
  • Insurance-based 3a locks you into fixed premiums for years, with a charge if you leave early
  • Over 30 years of maximum contributions, the gap between a 0% account and an ETF portfolio can exceed CHF 300,000
  • You can move your 3a balance to another provider at any time — tax-free
Option 1

Bank Savings Account

The most common default choice — and the least efficient for long-term growth.

A traditional bank 3a account works like a regular savings account. Your money earns interest — but at current Swiss rates, that interest is close to zero.

The tax deduction works exactly the same as with any other 3a. You contribute up to CHF 7,258 per year, and that amount comes off that year's taxable income — saving roughly CHF 1,000 to CHF 2,500 in tax for that year, depending on your income and canton, every year you contribute.

But the money sitting in the account grows very little. For a stay of two or three years, that may be acceptable. For anything longer, it is almost certainly not the most efficient choice.

Option 2

Insurance-Based Product

There is a strong sales industry behind this option: it's the one salespeople push hardest on newcomers — and the one that most often surprises expats later.

Insurance-based 3a products combine two things in one: a life insurance policy and an investment component — often in ETFs or funds. The pitch sounds attractive: insurance cover and investment growth at the same time. For most expats, the reality is more complicated.

How the sales industry works: insurance brokers and salespeople actively approach newcomers — often in their first weeks in the country, before they know how the system works — because every signed contract earns a commission. Once you sign, you are locked into a fixed payment for years. Yet the same result — the tax deduction and money invested in ETFs — is available with no obligation through Option 3: you open the account yourself on your phone and decide how much and when to contribute.

For most expats without dependants in Switzerland, the life insurance element adds cost without adding value.

Option 3

ETF Portfolio (Digital Providers)

The option that has grown fastest in recent years — and the one most financial comparisons recommend for long-term investors.

Instead of a savings account or an insurance policy, your 3a money goes into ETFs — funds that buy shares in hundreds or thousands of companies at once and simply follow world markets. That keeps both risk spread out and costs low.

The best-established low-cost providers in 2026 are finpension, VIAC and frankly. A newcomer, findependent, launched in October 2026 with an even lower fee, but has no track record yet. All of them open in under 10 minutes on a smartphone — no branch visit required.

Which Option Is Right for You?

Bank account can be enough

The simplicity and low commitment suit a short stay.

ETF account

An ETF-based digital account is almost certainly more efficient — the difference in returns over that period is meaningful. And you can withdraw everything when you leave Switzerland for good: no penalty, only a one-off withdrawal tax.

ETF accounts, split in several

An ETF-based digital setup spread across several accounts, so you can withdraw them in different years, is the strategy most financial experts recommend.

Review it first

Terms, fees and cancellation conditions vary significantly. Understanding what you signed is the first step — and an advisor can help you assess whether switching makes sense.

Can You Switch Providers?

Yes. Pillar 3a accounts are portable — you can transfer your balance from one provider to another at any time. The money goes directly between qualified 3a providers, you pay no tax on the transfer, and your deduction history is preserved.

Common Mistakes

01

Never reviewing the default bank account

Most expats open whatever account their bank offers and never think about it again. The cost of that inertia compounds every year.

02

Signing an insurance product without reading the terms

The fixed premium commitment and cancellation charges are often not fully explained at the point of sale. Read the full terms before signing.

03

Assuming all ETF providers are the same

finpension at 0.39% costs about 9% less than frankly at 0.43%. A few hundredths of a percent look trivial — but they are charged on your whole balance, every year, for decades.

04

Putting everything in one account

Withdrawals are taxed on a progressive scale: the more you take out in a single year, the higher the rate. An account must be withdrawn in full, so splitting your 3a across 4 to 5 accounts and withdrawing them over 4 to 5 years can save meaningfully on that tax.

Before You Ask

Which ETF provider is best for expats?▾
finpension and VIAC consistently rank as the top two options in 2026. VIAC offers more flexibility and a well-rated app. Both are strong choices for most expats.
I missed a year of contributions. Can I catch up?▾
Since 2026, yes — but only for gaps from 2025 onward, and only up to ten years back. A catch-up payment is deductible on top of that year's normal CHF 7,258. Gaps from 2024 and earlier cannot be filled.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
Before you leave Switzerland. Your 3a can be withdrawn when you leave for good, but the withdrawal tax depends on timing and on the canton where the provider's foundation is based — and good planning can reduce it.

Choosing the right Pillar 3a structure is one of those decisions that is easy to get right from the start — and surprisingly costly to get wrong. Many expats discover years later that a different provider or structure would have delivered significantly better results, with no additional risk or effort.
