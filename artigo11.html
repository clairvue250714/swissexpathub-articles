Pillar 3a: What It Is, How It Works, and Why Every Expat Should Open One

Quick Answer: Pillar 3a is Switzerland's voluntary private pension account. Every franc you contribute is deducted from your taxable income. For an expat earning CHF 120,000 in Geneva, contributing the maximum CHF 7,258 saves roughly CHF 2,100 in tax — this year, every year. You can open one at any Swiss bank. Deadline: 31 December.

Key Takeaways

  • Pillar 3a contributions are fully deductible from taxable income
  • 2026 limit: CHF 7,258 for employees, CHF 36,288 for self-employed
  • New in 2026: retroactive contributions possible for gaps from 2025 onwards
  • Gains inside Pillar 3a are tax-free until withdrawal
  • Available to all B and C permit holders with Swiss earned income

Switzerland's Three Pillars

Pillar 1

AHV/AVS State Pension

Mandatory. Funded by payroll deductions. Covers basic needs in retirement.

Pillar 2

BVG/LPP Occupational

Mandatory for employees. Managed by employer's pension fund.

Pillar 3a ← You decide

Private Pension

Voluntary. Fully tax-deductible. You choose the bank and investments.

Pillars 1 and 2 together replace roughly 60–70% of your last salary. Pillar 3a closes the gap — and gives you a tax deduction every year while doing it.1

The Rules in Plain Language

Who can contribute?

Anyone with Swiss-taxable earned income — employees, self-employed, B and C permit holders, cross-border workers who opt for ordinary assessment.

How much?

Employees with Pillar 2: CHF 7,258/year (2026). Self-employed without Pillar 2: up to 20% of net income, max CHF 36,288.

When is the deadline?

31 December of each year. Bank transfers take 1–2 days — don't leave it to the last minute.

When can I withdraw?

From age 60 (5 years before retirement). Earlier withdrawal possible when leaving Switzerland permanently, buying a primary residence, or starting a business.

Are investment gains taxed?

No. Capital gains and interest inside Pillar 3a are tax-free until withdrawal. At withdrawal, a reduced flat tax applies (varies by canton).

Can I have multiple accounts?

Yes. You can split contributions across up to 5 separate Pillar 3a accounts. This reduces the tax hit on withdrawal by staggering them across different years.

🆕 New in 2026: Retroactive Contributions

Starting January 2026, you can top up missed Pillar 3a contributions from previous years — up to 10 years back. Important: this only applies to gaps from 2025 onwards. Missed contributions from 2024 and earlier cannot be recovered. You must first pay the full 2026 contribution (CHF 7,258) before making any retroactive top-up.2

Before You Ask

I pay withholding tax (impôt à la source). Can I still use Pillar 3a?▾
Yes, but you need to file a tax return to claim the deduction. If you earn above CHF 120,000 (B permit), you must file anyway. Below that threshold, you can request ordinary assessment to claim the Pillar 3a deduction — but once you opt in, you cannot go back to withholding tax.
What happens to my Pillar 3a if I leave Switzerland?▾
You can withdraw it when you permanently leave Switzerland. A withholding tax is applied at the cantonal rate — this varies significantly by canton, which is why some expats transfer their 3a to a canton with a low exit tax before leaving (e.g. Schwyz or Appenzell).
Bank 3a or investment 3a — which is better?▾
The tax deduction is identical. The difference is return. A bank account earns minimal interest. An investment account (e.g. VIAC, Frankly, finpension) puts your money in equity funds — historically returning 5–7% per year. For expats staying 3+ years, investment 3a generally makes more sense.
Can my spouse also contribute?▾
Yes. Each working spouse has their own CHF 7,258 limit. A dual-income couple can contribute CHF 14,516 together and save CHF 3,000–5,000 in tax depending on income and canton.
