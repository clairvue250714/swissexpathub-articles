Cryptocurrency and Swiss Taxes: 5 Things Expats Often Miss

Quick Answer: Many expats arrive in Switzerland already owning cryptocurrency and assume that because it was purchased abroad, it has nothing to do with Swiss taxes. That assumption is worth revisiting. Switzerland is generally considered crypto-friendly. But "friendly" does not mean "invisible." The question is not whether you have a problem — it is whether everything is properly documented and reported.

Key Takeaways

  • Crypto held on foreign exchanges may still be relevant for Swiss tax reporting
  • Wealth tax can apply to crypto holdings — not just income tax
  • The distinction between private investor and professional trader matters
  • Poor record keeping is the most common problem — and the easiest to fix early
  • The larger your portfolio, the more a one-time review tends to be worth it

Mistake #1: Assuming Foreign Crypto Is Outside the Swiss System

Many expats continue using exchanges they opened before arriving — Coinbase, Binance, Kraken, or others. The reasoning is understandable: the account is foreign, the crypto was purchased before moving, and nobody has asked about it. However, once you become a Swiss tax resident, your worldwide assets become relevant for reporting purposes. The location of the exchange does not change that.

Mistake #2: Only Thinking About Capital Gains

Most crypto investors focus on one question: "Do I owe tax on my profits?" In Switzerland, the answer for most private investors is more nuanced than in many other countries. But focusing only on capital gains means overlooking something equally important. Wealth tax applies to the total value of your crypto holdings at year-end — regardless of whether you sold anything.

Mistake #3: Underestimating the Record Keeping Problem

This is the most common practical issue. Years after buying crypto, many investors struggle to reconstruct original purchase dates and prices, transfers between wallets, full exchange history, and year-end portfolio valuations. Swiss tax reporting requires declaring the value of holdings at the end of each tax year. The longer you wait to organize your records, the harder it becomes.

Mistake #4: Assuming All Investors Are Treated the Same

Swiss tax authorities distinguish between private investors and professional traders. For most expats holding crypto as a long-term investment, private investor status is typical. But certain patterns — frequent trading, use of leverage, short holding periods, or crypto representing a large portion of income — can change that assessment.

Mistake #5: Waiting Until Tax Season to Think About It

Most people only start researching crypto tax obligations when preparing their annual return. By then, records may be incomplete, planning opportunities may be gone, and fixing problems becomes more complicated than preventing them.

I have CHF 50,000 (or CHF 200,000) in crypto. Should I be concerned? Not necessarily. Owning cryptocurrency does not mean you have a tax problem. But it raises questions worth answering honestly: Do you know the value of your holdings at year-end? Are your purchase records complete? Have you considered how crypto affects wealth tax reporting?

Before You Ask

Do I need to declare crypto held on foreign exchanges?▾
In most cases, yes. Swiss tax residents are generally required to declare worldwide assets, including crypto held on foreign platforms. The location of the exchange does not eliminate the reporting obligation.
Does crypto affect wealth tax?▾
Yes. Cryptocurrency is treated as a taxable asset for wealth tax purposes. Holdings must typically be declared at their year-end market value. Valuation rules can be complex for less liquid tokens.
What if I have held crypto for many years without reporting it?▾
This situation is more common than most people realize. There are legal mechanisms in Switzerland to regularize undeclared assets. Speaking with an advisor before the situation becomes a formal problem is strongly recommended.
What is the most common mistake?▾
Poor record keeping. Many investors discover years later that transaction history is incomplete, wallet transfers are undocumented, or year-end valuations were never recorded. These gaps are much easier to prevent than to fix.
