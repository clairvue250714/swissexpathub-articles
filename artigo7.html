US Citizens in Switzerland: What You Need to Know About Your Tax Obligations

Quick Answer: The United States is one of only two countries in the world that taxes its citizens based on citizenship rather than residence. As an American living in Switzerland, you have tax obligations in both countries simultaneously — regardless of how long you have lived abroad. Most Americans in Geneva know they pay Swiss taxes. Fewer are confident they are handling their US obligations correctly.

Key Takeaways

  • US citizens must file a US tax return every year — regardless of where they live
  • Swiss bank accounts and assets must be reported to the IRS under FBAR and FATCA rules
  • Several tools exist to reduce or eliminate double taxation — but they must be actively claimed
  • Swiss investment funds are often classified as PFICs, which creates significant US tax complexity
  • A new US-Switzerland FATCA agreement (June 2024) moves to automatic data exchange — the IRS is likely already receiving information about your Swiss accounts
  • Americans who have not filed US returns can often catch up without penalties through specific IRS procedures

Your Three Core Obligations

Obligation 1

File a US Tax Return Every Year

US tax obligations apply regardless of where you live. Even with zero US tax due, Form 1040 must be filed annually. The filing deadline for Americans abroad is automatically extended to June 15. A further extension to October 15 is available on request.

This applies even if: you have lived outside the US for many years; you have no US income; you already pay full Swiss taxes; you have never owned property in the US.

Obligation 2

Report Foreign Bank Accounts — FBAR

An FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) must be filed if your foreign accounts exceed $10,000 in aggregate at any point during the year. This includes your Swiss current account, savings account, brokerage accounts, and pension funds in some cases. Swiss banks share data with the IRS under FATCA — non-disclosure is not an option.

The FBAR is filed separately from the tax return, with a deadline of April 15 and an automatic extension to October 15. Penalties for non-filing reach up to $16,536 per violation for non-wilful violations, and up to $165,353 or 50% of the account balance for wilful violations.

Obligation 3

Report Foreign Assets — FATCA Form 8938

Separate from the FBAR, Form 8938 must be attached to your US return if foreign financial assets exceed certain thresholds. For US persons resident in Switzerland: more than $200,000 at year-end or $300,000 at any point for single filers; more than $400,000 at year-end or $600,000 at any point for married couples filing jointly.

FBAR and Form 8938 overlap — but are not the same. Both may need to be filed.

The Tools to Reduce Double Taxation

Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)

Allows qualifying US citizens to exclude a portion of foreign earned income from US taxable income. For 2025: up to $130,000 per person. For 2026: $132,900. To qualify, you must meet either the Physical Presence Test (at least 330 full days abroad in a 12-month period) or the Bona Fide Residence Test (resident of Switzerland for a full calendar year). The FEIE is not automatic — it must be elected by filing Form 2555.

Foreign Tax Credit

Taxes paid to Switzerland can be credited against US tax owed — reducing or eliminating double taxation on income above the FEIE threshold or on passive income such as dividends and interest. A common approach: use the FEIE for earned salary up to the limit, then the Foreign Tax Credit for income above that threshold. You cannot apply both to the same dollar of income.

What If You Have Not Filed in Several Years?

This is more common than most people realise — and it is addressable. The IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures allow eligible taxpayers to file three years of past returns and six years of FBARs, free of penalties. This option is only available to those who have not been contacted by the IRS about missing returns. Acting proactively — before the IRS contacts you — is always the better approach.

The FATCA Agreement: Why Transparency Is Increasing

Switzerland and the US signed an updated FATCA agreement in June 2024, shifting to automatic mutual exchange of financial account data. Swiss financial institutions will report account data to the Swiss Federal Tax Administration, which then transmits it to the IRS. This change is expected to come into effect in 2027.

In practical terms: the IRS is already receiving significant information about Swiss accounts. The window for assuming non-disclosure goes unnoticed is closing.

Common Mistakes

Assuming Swiss taxes replace US obligations

They do not. Both sets of obligations exist simultaneously and must be managed together.

Filing a US return but not the FBAR

Many Americans file Form 1040 annually but are unaware that the FBAR is a separate filing with its own deadline and its own penalties.

Investing in Swiss funds without understanding PFIC rules

One of the most expensive mistakes to discover late. Restructuring a portfolio after the fact is far more complex than getting the structure right from the start.

Waiting too long to address missed filings

The Streamlined Procedures are only available before the IRS initiates contact. Acting early preserves options that disappear once enforcement begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I really need to file a US return if I pay full Swiss taxes?▾
Yes. The US filing obligation is based on citizenship, not on where taxes are paid. Swiss taxes do not replace US filing requirements — though they can often be credited against US tax owed.
What happens if I have never filed an FBAR?▾
The IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures may allow you to catch up on missed FBARs without penalties, provided the IRS has not already contacted you. Professional guidance is strongly recommended before using this process.
Are my Swiss pension contributions recognised by the IRS?▾
The IRS does not treat Pillar 2 and Pillar 3 contributions as tax-deferred in the same way US retirement accounts are treated. This can create unexpected US tax obligations on contributions that are fully tax-efficient in Switzerland.
Should I use the FEIE or the Foreign Tax Credit?▾
It depends on your specific situation — income level, type of income, Swiss tax paid, and other factors. The choice can have significant long-term consequences and is one of the most important decisions in US expat tax planning.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
As soon as possible — and ideally before making investment decisions, before missing another filing deadline, or before assuming your current approach is correct.
