Early Retirement in Switzerland: What Expats Need to Know About FIRE

FIRE stands for Financial Independence, Retire Early: saving enough to live off your investments and stop working well before 65.

Quick Answer: In Switzerland, FIRE needs two pots. Free capital to live on until 60, and your pension pillars (Pillar 2 and Pillar 3a), which only unlock from 60 — or earlier if you leave the country. The plan changes a lot depending on whether you stay or go.

Key Takeaways

  • The classic target is 25 times what you spend a year: spending CHF 60,000 (CHF 5,000 a month) means you need CHF 1,500,000
  • Moving to the UK or the US lets you withdraw your whole Pillar 2; moving to an EU country and working there leaves the mandatory part locked until retirement
  • Withdrawing Pillar 2 and Pillar 3a in different years cuts the tax: in Geneva, splitting CHF 500,000 over two years saves about CHF 5,900
  • Withdrawal tax depends on the canton you live in on the day you withdraw: CHF 250,000 costs about CHF 15,500 in Geneva and about CHF 11,500 in Zug
  • If you stay in Switzerland, you keep paying wealth tax — and AHV contributions (the state pension) until 65
#1

The 25× Rule — Where to Start

The most widely used FIRE rule: save 25 times your annual spending. It comes from US studies: withdrawing 4% of your capital a year, adjusted for inflation, made the money last at least 30 years in the vast majority of historical scenarios. Since 1 ÷ 4% = 25, the target is 25×.

If you stop at 45, the money has to last 40 years or more. That's why many people use a more cautious 3.5% withdrawal rate — which raises the target to about 28.6× your annual spending.

#2

The Pillar 2 Problem — The Biggest Obstacle

Pillar 2 is your employer's compulsory pension fund: you and your employer pay into it every month. Over a career it builds up a lot of money — but that money is locked.

When you stop working before retirement age, the balance moves to a vested benefits account (compte de libre passage) — a holding account at a bank or foundation where the money keeps growing. In Switzerland, you can only withdraw it from age 60 (5 years before the reference age of 65).

#3

Pillar 3a — The FIRE Strategy

Pillar 3a is private pension saving with a tax deduction (up to CHF 7,258 in 2026 — about CHF 605 a month — if you have a Pillar 2). The withdrawal rules are the same as for Pillar 2, with one advantage: when you leave Switzerland, you can take out all of your 3a, even if you move to the EU.

Note: once you stop working, you can no longer pay in — Pillar 3a requires earned income.

Why several accounts: one withdrawal a year

Each 3a account must be withdrawn in full, in one go. With several accounts you can take one a year. The law sets no limit on the number of accounts; five is practical, because it fits one a year between 60 and 65.

Watch out: withdrawal tax is progressive — the more you take out in one year, the higher the rate. Everything you withdraw in the same calendar year (Pillar 2, Pillar 3a and, if you're married, your spouse's) is added together and taxed as one.

Rules unchanged: in March 2026, Parliament rejected the proposal to raise the tax on these withdrawals.

#4

The Canton Strategy — Where You Live on Withdrawal Day

If you live in Switzerland when you withdraw, you pay the tax of the canton you live in on that day. The differences between cantons are large.

Tax on a Pillar 2 / 3a withdrawal — single, 2026

Federal, cantonal and municipal tax, cantonal capital, no church tax. Source: Federal Tax Administration (ESTV) calculator.

Zug and Appenzell Innerrhoden are the cheapest in the table at every amount. Mind the name: Appenzell Ausserrhoden is one of the most expensive (7.94% already at CHF 100,000).

Moving canton just to withdraw: the move has to be real and lasting. If you move back soon after, your old canton may claim the tax.

Leaving the country: if you already live abroad on withdrawal day, Switzerland charges a withholding tax at the rate of the canton where the foundation holding your money has its seat — not the canton you lived in. That's why some people move their Pillar 3a and vested benefits account to a foundation based in a low-withholding-tax canton before leaving. Depending on the tax treaty with your new country, this tax may be refunded — but your new country may tax the withdrawal.

    Before You Ask

    Are ETFs a good FIRE vehicle in Switzerland?▾
    Yes. ETFs are exchange-traded funds that bundle hundreds of shares into one purchase. In Switzerland, gains from selling shares and ETFs held as private wealth are tax-free. Dividends, however, count as income, and the whole portfolio counts towards wealth tax. If you buy and sell very often, you may be treated as a professional trader and taxed on your gains.

    For US citizens: avoid ETFs domiciled outside the US. Under the PFIC rules (the US rules for foreign funds), they are taxed punitively in the US. Use US-domiciled funds through a broker that accepts US clients.
    When should I speak with an advisor?▾
    Before any major decision — especially about withdrawing Pillar 2, changing canton or leaving the country. A plan made 5–10 years before your target date is far more effective than one made at the last minute.
