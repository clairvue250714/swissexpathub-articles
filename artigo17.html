Self-Employed in Switzerland: Tax Basics for Expats

Quick Answer: No one withholds anything for you — you calculate, declare and pay your income tax and AHV yourself. Set aside 30–35% of every invoice from day one. In return, without a Pillar 2 pension you can deduct far more through Pillar 3a than an employee can.

Key Takeaways

  • AHV costs 10% of net income from CHF 60,500 a year — with no employer to share it
  • Your Pillar 3a cap is 20% of net income, up to CHF 36,288 — on CHF 150,000, that is CHF 30,000
  • Working for a single client, on their premises, can get you reclassified as an employee
  • Running a foreign company from Switzerland can create a permanent establishment here
  • VAT registration is mandatory from CHF 100,000 of worldwide turnover — foreign clients count

#1

Who Counts as Self-Employed in Switzerland

In Switzerland, you are considered self-employed if you work independently, bear your own financial risk and are not subordinate to a single client.

The authorities look at the substance of your work, not just the contract. If you work exclusively for one client, have no other clients and work on their premises with their equipment, you may be classified as an employee — whatever your contract says. That reclassification creates large retroactive tax and AHV liabilities.

#2

AHV: The Contribution Most Expats Underestimate

This is the single most common financial surprise for newly self-employed expats in Switzerland.

Self-employed people pay AHV/IV/EO at 10% of net income (8.1% AHV + 1.4% IV + 0.5% EO) — with no employer to share the cost. The full rate applies from CHF 60,500 a year; below that, a lower rate applies (sliding scale). Below CHF 10,100, you pay the minimum contribution of CHF 530 a year. On top, the compensation fund charges administrative costs and the family allowance contribution.

You register with your cantonal AHV compensation fund as soon as you start. Contributions are paid quarterly in advance, based on an estimate, then adjusted. If you underestimate your income, you face a large catch-up payment. So set aside 30–35% of your income from day one — AHV and back taxes can be heavy if not planned for.

#3

Income Tax for the Self-Employed

Your business income is added to your personal income and taxed at the same progressive rates as any other resident. You file a full annual tax return and declare your business income, expenses and deductions yourself. There is no withholding at source.

In Geneva, the combined top marginal rate (cantonal, communal and federal) can approach 45% on high incomes. AHV contributions are deductible from taxable income — which lowers your effective rate.

#4

The Pillar 3a Advantage for the Self-Employed

This is one of the most valuable — and least used — tools for self-employed expats.

Without a Pillar 2 pension, you can contribute up to 20% of net income, capped at CHF 36,288 a year, and deduct all of it from taxable income. To reach the cap, your net income must be at least CHF 181,440 (36,288 ÷ 20%).

On a net income of CHF 150,000 in Geneva: 150,000 × 20% = CHF 30,000 maximum contribution. At a 35–40% marginal rate, that saves around CHF 10,500 to CHF 12,000 in tax in a single year.

#5

If You Have a Foreign Company

Many expats keep working through a company registered in another country — the UK, Ireland, France or elsewhere. This adds complexity.

Permanent establishmentIf you run the foreign company from Switzerland — signing contracts, making decisions, serving clients — the Swiss tax authorities may find it has a permanent establishment here. Profits linked to the Swiss activity are then taxed here.
Personal incomeAs a Swiss resident, you are taxed here on your worldwide income. Salary or dividends you draw from the foreign company go into your Swiss return, wherever the company is registered.
VATIf the worldwide turnover of all your activities exceeds CHF 100,000 a year, you must register for Swiss VAT — even if all your clients are abroad. Many expats find out late and have to register retroactively.
#6

Sole Trader or Company?

Most self-employed expats start as sole traders — the simplest structure, with no minimum capital and straightforward taxation. As income grows, some consider a GmbH (Sàrl), Switzerland's limited liability company.

For most expats earning under CHF 200,000, a sole trader structure is simpler and often more tax-efficient — as long as Pillar 3a deductions are fully used.

Before You Ask

Can I deduct business expenses?▾
Yes. Legitimate business expenses — equipment, professional subscriptions, home office costs, business travel, professional training — are deductible from business income before tax.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
Before you start — or as soon as you arrive in Switzerland with existing self-employed income. The decisions made in your first year set the foundation for everything that follows.

Self-employment in Switzerland rewards those who plan ahead. With AHV, income tax, Pillar 3a and, for many expats, a foreign company in the mix, professional advice typically pays for itself many times over.
