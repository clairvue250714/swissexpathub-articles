Self-Employed in Switzerland: Tax Basics for Expats

Quick Answer: Being self-employed in Switzerland gives you significant flexibility — and significant responsibility. Unlike an employee, no one withholds your taxes or social security contributions. For most expats, the biggest surprise is not the income tax — it is the AHV social security contribution that arrives on top of it. Set aside 30–35% of your income from day one.

Key Takeaways

  • Self-employed persons pay AHV at ~10% of net income — with no employer to share the cost
  • Set aside 30–35% of your income from day one — taxes plus AHV can easily reach that level
  • Without a Pillar 2 pension, you can contribute up to CHF 36,288 to Pillar 3a and deduct it in full
  • If you have a foreign company and live in Switzerland, permanent establishment rules may apply
  • VAT registration is mandatory if your worldwide turnover exceeds CHF 100,000

AHV — The Contribution Most Expats Underestimate

This is the single most common financial surprise for newly self-employed expats. If you are self-employed, you pay AHV/IV/EO at ~10% of your net earned income. There is no employer to share the cost — you pay the full amount yourself. On a net income of CHF 150,000 — that is CHF 15,000 in AHV contributions alone, before income tax. Set aside 30–35% of your income from day one — AHV and back-taxes can be substantial if not planned for.

Income Tax for the Self-Employed

Your business income is added to your personal income and taxed at the same progressive rates as any other resident. You file a full annual tax return and declare your business income, expenses, and deductions yourself. There is no withholding at source. In Geneva, combined cantonal and federal income tax rates for self-employed persons with high income can reach 45%.

The Pillar 3a Advantage for the Self-Employed

Self-employed persons without a Pillar 2 pension can contribute up to CHF 36,288 or 20% of net earned income — whichever is lower — to Pillar 3a and deduct it in full from taxable income. On a net income of CHF 150,000 in Geneva — contributing the maximum CHF 36,288 to Pillar 3a saves approximately CHF 14,500 in tax in a single year.

If You Have a Foreign Company

Permanent establishment risk: If you manage your foreign company from Switzerland — signing contracts, making decisions, conducting client work — Swiss tax authorities may determine that your company has a permanent establishment in Switzerland. This means the company's profits attributable to Swiss activities are taxable here.

VAT obligation: If your worldwide turnover from all activities exceeds CHF 100,000, you must register for Swiss VAT — even if all your clients are outside Switzerland. The threshold applies globally, not just to Swiss revenue.

Expat A — Freelance consultant, CHF 120,000

No foreign company. Contributes CHF 24,000 to Pillar 3a (20% of net income). Deducts AHV contributions.

Effective taxable income after deductions: approximately CHF 82,000. Tax situation manageable with good planning.
Expat B — UK e-commerce agency, CHF 350,000

Lives in Switzerland with a C Permit. Revenue from UK clients. Has not registered for Swiss VAT. Has not assessed permanent establishment risk.

Before You Ask

How do I register as self-employed in Switzerland?▾
You register with the cantonal AHV compensation office. In Geneva, this is the OCAS. You declare your expected annual income and pay quarterly AHV advance payments. At year end, the actual contribution is calculated based on your tax return and any difference is settled.
Do I need to register for VAT?▾
If your worldwide turnover from self-employed activities exceeds CHF 100,000 in any 12-month period — yes. This includes revenue from foreign clients. The threshold applies globally, not just to Swiss revenue.
Can I deduct business expenses?▾
Yes. Legitimate business expenses — equipment, professional subscriptions, home office costs, business travel, professional training — are deductible from business income before tax.
I have a C permit. Can I be self-employed in Switzerland?▾
Yes. C permit holders have the same rights as Swiss nationals regarding employment — including the right to be self-employed. B permit holders may also be self-employed, but must demonstrate a viable business plan and financial stability when renewing their permit.
