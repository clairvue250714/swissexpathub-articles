Double Taxation Between the US and Switzerland: What Americans Need to Know

Quick Answer: No — if you use the right tools, an American in Switzerland does not pay tax twice on the same income. In most cases, either your income is excluded in the US, or the Swiss tax you have already paid is subtracted from your US tax — and what you owe the IRS (the US tax authority) is little or nothing. The real risk is not double taxation itself: it is filing in the US without claiming the protections you are entitled to — and overpaying for years without noticing.

Key Takeaways

  • Up to $132,900 of your salary (2026) can be left out of US tax entirely — this is the FEIE, claimed on Form 2555
  • On the rest of your income, Swiss tax already paid is subtracted from your US tax (the Foreign Tax Credit) — but never both tools on the same dollar
  • Social security: the Totalization Agreement stops you paying into both countries at once
  • Relying on the treaty means filing Form 8833 — forgetting it costs $1,000 per return
  • The treaty does not replace your annual US return, nor the duty to report your Swiss accounts and assets to the US (FBAR and FATCA)

The Starting Point: Two Countries, Two Sets of Obligations

As a US citizen or Green Card holder living in Switzerland, you have tax obligations in both countries at the same time.

Swiss federal income tax tops out at 11.5% — less than a third of the 37% top US federal rate. The gap narrows once cantonal, communal and wealth taxes are added, but for most expats the total burden is still lower than a comparable US state-plus-federal combination. That is why, used properly, the tools below usually leave little or nothing to pay the IRS.

Tool 1

The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)

In plain terms: the US lets people who live and work abroad take part of their salary out of the US tax calculation. That part is treated as if it did not exist for the IRS.

The limit is $130,000 for 2025 and $132,900 for 2026, per person. In a couple where both spouses qualify, each claims their own — up to $260,000 combined for 2025.

→ The $17,100 goes to Tool 2

To qualify, you must live abroad for a full calendar year, or spend at least 330 days outside the US in a 12-month period. It only covers earned income: salaries, freelance fees and business income — rent, dividends, capital gains and pensions are excluded. And it is not automatic: you claim it on Form 2555, filed with your annual return.

Tool 2

The Foreign Tax Credit

In plain terms: the tax you have already paid in Switzerland is subtracted from the tax you would owe the US. If the Swiss tax is equal or higher, you pay nothing extra to the IRS on that income.

This is where everything the FEIE does not cover goes — salary above the limit and investment income.

The most common approach is to combine the two: the FEIE for salary up to the limit, the Foreign Tax Credit for the rest. The one rule that cannot be broken: the two never apply to the same dollar of income.

Tool 3

The Totalization Agreement — Social Security

In plain terms: it makes sure you only pay into one country's pension and social security system, not both.

What the Treaty Does Not Cover

✕Your annual US return

You still have to file a US return every year, even with nothing to pay. The treaty reduces the tax, not the paperwork.

✕Reporting your accounts and assets abroad

Two separate obligations — they are not taxes, they are reports:

FBARAn annual list of your bank accounts outside the US, filed with the US Treasury whenever their combined total goes above $10,000 at any point in the year.
FATCAA US law that requires banks worldwide — Swiss banks included — to report American clients' accounts to the IRS. On your side, once your foreign assets pass certain thresholds, you list them on a dedicated form (Form 8938).

Not reporting carries heavy fines, even with no tax owed. We cover both in detail in our article on FBAR and FATCA.

✕Telling the IRS you're using the treaty

Whenever you pay less US tax because of a treaty article, you must say so on a dedicated form, Form 8833. Forgetting it costs:

$0per return — even with no tax due

Common Mistakes

1

Using the FEIE and the Foreign Tax Credit on the same income

One of the most frequent errors on US expat returns — and it can lead the IRS to adjust your return, with tax and interest to pay.

2

Choosing the FEIE without thinking long-term

Once chosen, the FEIE carries over from year to year. If you ever revoke it — for example, to switch to the Foreign Tax Credit only — you cannot choose it again for five years without IRS approval. The first choice deserves careful thought.

Before You Ask

I have a Green Card, not citizenship. Does this apply to me?▾
Yes. As long as you hold the Green Card, you are treated as a US tax resident and have the same filing obligations — and the same tools — as a citizen.
Do I have to claim the FEIE every year?▾
The choice stays in place until you revoke it, but Form 2555 must be filed with your return for every year in which you want to use the exclusion.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
If you have never checked whether you are using both tools correctly, if your income combines salary and investments, or before choosing between the FEIE and the Foreign Tax Credit for the first time.
