Moving to Switzerland: Financial Checklist for Expats

Quick Answer: Switzerland has two deadlines that define your first months — and missing either one is expensive.

Beyond that, the financial decisions you make in the first few weeks after arrival have a larger long-term impact than most newcomers realise — from which canton you live in to how you set up your banking and pension contributions. This article covers the financial essentials for anyone arriving in Switzerland.

Key Takeaways

  • Register with your municipality within 14 days of arrival — this triggers the creation of your B or L permit
  • Sign up for health insurance within 90 days — missing this deadline means being assigned a random provider with premiums backdated to your arrival date
  • Income and wealth taxes can double or even triple depending on the canton and municipality where you settle
  • You need a Swiss bank account almost immediately — your employer, landlord, and insurers all expect a Swiss IBAN
  • Starting Pillar 3a contributions early is one of the best financial decisions a new arrival can make
#1

The Two Deadlines That Define Your First Months

14 days: Register with your municipality

You must register at the cantonal immigration office within 14 days of arrival to obtain a residence permit — such as a Permit L or Permit B, depending on your circumstances.

This is not optional and not negotiable. Registration triggers everything else — your permit, your tax file, and the clock on your health insurance deadline.

90 days: Sign up for health insurance

Swiss health insurance (KVG/LAMal) is mandatory for everyone living in Switzerland, regardless of nationality. If you miss the deadline, authorities will assign you to a random provider and backdate premiums to your arrival date — you will receive a very unpleasant bill.

In 2026, the average basic premium for an adult is around CHF 465 per month (the overall average, which includes children and young adults, is CHF 393). Choose your deductible wisely — higher deductible means lower monthly premium but more out-of-pocket when you visit a doctor. Deductibles range from CHF 300 to CHF 2,500.

Health insurance is not provided by your employer in Switzerland — it is your individual responsibility.

#2

The Canton Decision: More Important Than Most People Realise

Where you choose to live in Switzerland is one of the most significant financial decisions you will make — and most newcomers underestimate it.

The tax difference between a low-tax canton like Zug and Geneva can exceed CHF 30,000 per year on a high income. Income and wealth taxes can double or even triple depending on the canton and municipality where you settle.

If you are working in Zurich or Geneva, you do not necessarily need to live there. Many expats commute from lower-tax cantons — though the trade-off between tax savings and lifestyle must be considered carefully.

For those already committed to a location, understanding the local tax rates and available deductions is far more useful than comparing cantons after the fact.

#3

Opening a Swiss Bank Account

You need a Swiss bank account almost immediately after arrival.

Your employer will deposit your salary into a Swiss IBAN. Your landlord will expect rent payments from a Swiss account. Health insurance premiums, utility bills, and tax payments all run smoother with a local account. Some landlords and service providers will not accept foreign bank transfers.

For Americans, opening a Swiss bank account requires additional disclosure steps due to FATCA obligations. Some banks are more experienced with US clients than others — this is worth researching before choosing a provider.

#4

Starting Pillar 3a Early

One of the best financial decisions a new arrival can make is to open a Pillar 3a account as soon as possible.

Every year you contribute the maximum — CHF 7,258 for employees in 2026 — is a year where your Swiss income tax bill is reduced by approximately CHF 2,000 to CHF 2,500 in a mid-to-high tax canton.

Starting in year one rather than year three means two additional years of tax savings and two additional years of compound growth on the invested funds.

#5

Understanding Your Permit and What It Means Financially

Your permit type affects your tax situation more than most newcomers realise.

With a B Permit, taxes are typically deducted directly from your salary (tax at source). If your gross income reaches CHF 120,000 a year, a full tax return becomes mandatory.

Below that threshold, you can request a full tax return by 31 March of the following year — this is how you claim deductions such as Pillar 3a contributions or pension fund buy-ins. The deadline is strict: miss it, and tax at source is final for that year.

With a C Permit, tax at source ends and you file every year like any Swiss resident. Understanding this transition early helps you plan for it.

#6

For Americans: Additional Steps From Day One

If you are a US citizen or Green Card holder, moving to Switzerland does not suspend your US tax obligations. Several actions should be taken from your first year:

FBARConfirm your FBAR obligations — your Swiss bank account almost certainly triggers the filing requirement.
FEIE or Foreign Tax CreditChoose on your first Swiss-year US return. With Swiss tax levels, the credit is often the better option — and the FEIE election is hard to reverse.
FATCAInform your Swiss bank of your US person status — this is required under FATCA.
Certificate of CoverageIf you were seconded by a US employer, confirm it has applied for one under the Totalization Agreement. Hired locally? You pay into Swiss AHV as normal.

These obligations are covered in detail in our dedicated articles on US taxation in Switzerland.

Common Mistakes

01

Missing the health insurance deadline

If you miss the 90-day deadline, authorities will force-assign you to a random provider and backdate your premiums — with a penalty surcharge. This is one of the most avoidable and most common errors among new arrivals.

02

Assuming the canton does not matter

Many expats choose where to live based on proximity to work and housing costs — without considering the tax implications. The difference can be substantial over a multi-year stay.

03

Delaying Pillar 3a

Since 2026, missed Pillar 3a contributions can be made up retroactively — but with limits: only for gaps from 2025 onward, only after paying the current year's maximum, and up to CHF 7,258 per catch-up year. The compound growth of the missed years does not come back — starting early is still the best route.

04

Not reviewing US obligations from day one

For Americans, the first Swiss tax year sets patterns that are difficult to change later. Getting the foundations right from the start is far easier than correcting years of errors.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a Swiss bank account before I arrive?▾
Not necessarily — but you will need one within the first few weeks. Some digital banks allow you to open an account before arrival with a passport and Swiss address confirmation.
Can I keep my foreign health insurance when moving to Switzerland?▾
EU/EFTA nationals can use the European Health Insurance Card for short-term residency, but all long-term residents must obtain Swiss health insurance within three months of arrival. Foreign private health insurance does not generally substitute for the mandatory Swiss system.
Does it matter which canton I live in for tax purposes?▾
Significantly. The tax difference between low-tax and high-tax cantons can exceed CHF 30,000 per year on a high income. If you have flexibility in where you live, the canton decision deserves careful consideration before signing a rental contract.
When should I open a Pillar 3a account?▾
As soon as possible after arrival — ideally in your first year. Contributions must reach the account by December 31 to qualify as a deduction for that tax year.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
Ideally before or shortly after arrival — particularly if you are American, have assets in multiple countries, or want to ensure your tax position is optimised from the start rather than corrected later.
