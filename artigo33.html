Pillar 2: Your Swiss Pension Fund at Retirement — Buy-Ins, Lump Sum or Pension

Quick Answer: Pillar 2 is your employer's pension fund. Unlike AHV, the money sits in an account in your name — and if you arrived in Switzerland late, you can pay in extra and deduct it from your taxes. At retirement, you choose between taking it all at once or a monthly pension for life. And if you leave Switzerland for the UK or the US, you can take the money with you.

Key Takeaways

  • Your fund's annual certificate shows what you've saved and how much more you can pay in
  • Paying in extra is the only way to make up for missing AHV years
  • If you arrived less than five years ago, there's a yearly limit
  • Choosing between a lump sum and a pension requires advance notice to your fund

What Is Pillar 2?

Every employee earning more than CHF 22,680 a year joins their employer's pension fund. Every month, you and your employer — who pays at least half — put a percentage of your salary into an account in your name. The older you are, the higher the percentage. Each year, the fund sends you a certificate showing what you've saved and, often, how much more you can pay in.

The Buy-In: Paying In Extra

If you arrived mid-career, you have less in your fund than someone who worked here all their life. The law lets you close the gap with voluntary payments — a buy-in (Einkauf / rachat) — deducted from your taxable income that year. The most you can pay in is your buy-in capacity: what your fund says you should have by now, for your age and salary, minus what you actually have.

Example — Emma, from London

Emma is 42, moved from London to Geneva two years ago and earns CHF 120,000 a year. Her fund certificate shows:

What she should have
CHF 250,000
What she actually has
CHF 30,000
Buy-in capacity
CHF 220,000
  • What she should have: CHF 250,000 — what she'd have in Pillar 2 today if she'd worked in Switzerland since age 25 on her salary. Pillar 2 only starts saving for retirement at 25; before that, even people who already work are only covered for disability and death.
  • What she actually has: CHF 30,000 — just her two years in Geneva.
  • The difference is her buy-in capacity: 250,000 − 30,000 = CHF 220,000 she can pay in on top, deducted from her taxes.

But how much per year? In her first five years in the fund, at most 20% of her insured salary — not her full salary. Since AHV already covers the first part of your salary, the fund subtracts a fixed amount (the coordination deduction, CHF 26,460 in 2026) and only insures the rest: 120,000 − 26,460 = CHF 93,540. So: 93,540 × 20% ≈ CHF 18,700 a year, on top of the regular contributions already taken from her salary. The exact figure is on your certificate; some funds calculate it differently.

Buy-ins are usually paid in one go, often at the end of the year — the monthly figure is what she'd need to set aside. She puts about CHF 1,560 a month into the fund, but only about CHF 1,090 really comes out of her pocket. The other CHF 470 a month comes back as lower tax: when that year's tax return is assessed, the following year, her tax bill is lower by that amount.

Three Rules Before You Pay In

5

The first five years

If you came from abroad and were never in a Swiss fund, you can buy in at most 20% of your insured salary per year. After that, you can pay in the rest.

3

The three-year lock

After each buy-in, that money can't be taken out as a lump sum for three years — at retirement, to buy a home or when leaving Switzerland. Otherwise, the tax office can reverse the deduction.

⌂

Used it for a home?

If you've already used your fund to buy a home, you must pay that money back before you can make tax-deductible buy-ins.

Making Up for Missing AHV Years

First, a rule of thumb. Your fund turns your savings into a pension using the conversion rate — on average 5.2% a year in 2026 (yours is on your certificate). In practice:

In your fund at 65CHF 100,000→Pension per month≈ CHF 430100,000 × 5.2% = CHF 5,200 a year ÷ 12 ≈ CHF 430 a month
Example — how much to pay in to cover the AHV gap

An employee earns CHF 5,000 a month and will have 20 years of AHV. His AHV pension is about CHF 1,150 a month below a full one — and those years can't be bought (see Pillar 1).

  • How much extra he needs in the fund: to receive another CHF 1,150 a month, about CHF 265,000 (2.65 × CHF 430 ≈ CHF 1,150)
  • How much to pay in: CHF 265,000 over 20 years ≈ CHF 1,100 a month on average. In his first five years in the fund the limit is lower: (60,000 − 26,460) × 20% ≈ CHF 6,700 a year, about CHF 560 a month — the rest comes later
  • How much comes back in tax: on his salary the marginal rate is about 25%. Of the CHF 1,100 paid in each month, about CHF 275 comes back as lower tax → the real cost is about CHF 825 a month
  • And a little less still: the fund pays interest on what you put in

What Every CHF 1,000 Really Costs You

A buy-in comes off your taxable income. So part of what you pay in comes back as lower tax — the more, the higher your marginal rate (the rate on the last franc you earn).

Lump Sum or Pension

At retirement, you take Pillar 2 all at once (a lump sum), as a monthly pension for life, or a mix. You can ask for at least a quarter as a lump sum — many funds allow all of it — but you must give notice: many funds ask for up to three years.

Example — Sarah, from Manchester

Sarah retires in Geneva at 65 with CHF 400,000 in Pillar 2.

  • Pension: 400,000 × 5.2% = CHF 20,800 a year ÷ 12 ≈ CHF 1,730 a month, for life
  • Break-even: 400,000 ÷ 20,800 ≈ 19 years. If she lives beyond about 84, the pension pays more (ignoring interest and tax)

If You Leave Switzerland

UK · US

Take it all

Both are outside the EU/EFTA, so you can withdraw your whole Pillar 2. Switzerland withholds tax on the payout, which can usually be reclaimed under the double tax treaties.

EU / EFTA

Part stays

The compulsory part usually stays in Switzerland until retirement.

If you don't withdraw it, the money waits in a vested benefits account (Freizügigkeitskonto / compte de libre passage) in Switzerland until retirement.

Before You Ask

What happens to my Pillar 2 if I change jobs?▾
The money moves to your new employer's fund. Between jobs, it sits in a vested benefits account. Nothing is lost.
Can I use Pillar 2 to buy a home?▾
Yes, for a home you'll live in. But it reduces your future pension and is taxed when you take it out. Financing property in Switzerland explains how it works.
