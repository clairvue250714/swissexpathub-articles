Cost of Living in Geneva on CHF 80,000–120,000 a Year

Quick Answer: On CHF 100,000 gross, a single person takes home around CHF 6,700 a month and can save CHF 2,000–2,500. What decides the rest is rent, health insurance — among the most expensive in Switzerland, and paid entirely by you — and, if you have them, children.

Key Takeaways

  • Basic health insurance in Geneva averages around CHF 586 a month per adult, with no employer contribution
  • A one-bedroom apartment ranges from CHF 1,800 in the suburbs to CHF 2,500 in the city centre
  • One international school can consume 25–45% of a CHF 100,000 salary
  • Pillar 3a saves around CHF 2,400 in tax a year — but on a B Permit, only if you request a full tax return by 31 March

#2

Housing — The Largest Slice

Geneva consistently ranks among the most expensive cities in the world for rent. The Swiss rule of thumb is to spend no more than 30% of net income on housing: on CHF 100,000 gross (around CHF 6,700 net a month), that means a maximum rent of about CHF 2,000 a month.

#3

Health Insurance — The Surprise Most Expats Don't See Coming

Geneva has some of the most expensive health insurance in Switzerland — and there is no employer contribution. You pay the full amount yourself, every month, on top of everything else.

The average basic (LAMal) premium in Geneva is around CHF 586 a month per adult in 2026. For a couple, that is about CHF 1,172 a month — CHF 14,064 a year — before a single doctor's visit.

To pay less: choose a higher deductible (CHF 1,500 or CHF 2,500 a year instead of the minimum CHF 300) — the premium drops significantly, but you pay more out of pocket when you see a doctor. For young, healthy expats who rarely use healthcare, the higher deductible usually wins on total annual cost. HMO or Telmed models, where you see a GP or call a health line first, also lower the premium. Compare providers every year at priminfo.admin.ch.

Subsidy: on a lower income, you may qualify for a cantonal health insurance subsidy (subside LAMal) that covers part or all of your premium. Many expats never apply because they don't know it exists.

#4

Food

Geneva is expensive for groceries, but there are options for every budget. A realistic monthly budget: CHF 400–600 for a single person cooking at home. Add CHF 300–600 if you eat out regularly. A main course at a normal Geneva restaurant costs CHF 25–40; a coffee and croissant, CHF 8–12.

#5

Transport

Geneva has excellent public transport — trams, buses and lake boats run by TPG. A monthly Unireso pass for the whole canton costs CHF 70 (or CHF 510 a year if you pay annually).

For those who live outside Geneva and commute in, the national GA pass costs CHF 3,995 a year in 2nd class — rising to CHF 4,095 from December 2026. Many people who live in cheaper cantons use it.

A car in the city is rarely necessary and expensive to run: insurance CHF 1,200–2,000 a year, fuel around CHF 1.80 a litre, city parking CHF 3–6 an hour.

#8

Pillar 3a: The Best Return Available

Contributing the maximum to Pillar 3a (CHF 7,258 a year, about CHF 605 a month) saves around CHF 2,400 in tax a year in Geneva. In practice, setting aside CHF 605 a month and getting CHF 2,400 back in tax is the best return available in Switzerland.

On a B Permit, watch out: your tax is deducted at source and Pillar 3a is not taken into account automatically. With a salary below CHF 120,000, you must request a full tax return (TOU) by 31 March of the following year to deduct it. After that date, the request is not accepted.

Before You Ask

Is it worth living across the border in France?▾
For some expats, yes — rents are much lower in the French border area (Annemasse, Gex, Ferney-Voltaire). But you become a cross-border worker: you need a G Permit, you still pay tax in Geneva (deducted at source from your Swiss salary), and commuting takes time. It makes most sense for families who need space and cannot afford Geneva rents.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
When you want to optimise — Pillar 3a contributions, tax deductions, pension fund buy-ins. These decisions compound over time and are much easier to get right from the start than to correct years later.
