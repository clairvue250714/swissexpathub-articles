First: What Stays in Your Pocket

Gross salary in Switzerland is not what ends up in your bank account. Income tax, AHV social contributions, and Pillar 2 deductions take a meaningful share. The net figure depends on your canton, commune, family situation, and permit type.

Housing — The Largest Slice

Housing in Geneva is expensive. The city consistently ranks among the most expensive in the world for rent. The Swiss rule of thumb is to spend no more than 30% of net income on housing — on CHF 100,000 gross (~CHF 6,700 net/month), that means a maximum rent of around CHF 2,000/month.

Health Insurance — The Surprise Most Expats Don't See Coming

Geneva has the highest health insurance premiums in Switzerland — and there is no employer contribution. You pay the full amount yourself, every month, on top of everything else.

⚠ Average LAMal premium in Geneva 2026: CHF 562/month per adult. For a couple, that is CHF 1,124/month — CHF 13,488/year — before a single doctor's visit. Dental care, glasses for adults, and most alternative medicine are not covered by basic insurance.

Choosing a higher deductible (CHF 1,500 or CHF 2,500/year) reduces the monthly premium to CHF 400–480 — but you pay more out of pocket when you do visit a doctor. For young healthy expats who rarely use healthcare, the higher deductible usually wins on annual total cost.

✅ Subsidy check: If you earn below approximately CHF 60,000–80,000 gross, you may qualify for a cantonal health insurance subsidy (subside LAMal/IPV). Geneva's subsidy programme is Switzerland's most generous — it can cover part or all of your premium. Many expats miss this entirely because they never knew to apply.

Food

Geneva is expensive for groceries — but has options for every budget.

Lidl and Aldi are the cheapest but have fewer locations. Migros and Coop are the everyday Swiss supermarkets — reliable quality, reasonable prices, everywhere. Manor Food and Globus are premium.

A realistic monthly food budget: CHF 400–600/month for a single person cooking at home. Add CHF 300–600 if you eat out regularly. A main course at a normal Geneva restaurant costs CHF 25–40. A coffee and croissant: CHF 8–12.

💡 The France trick: Many expats cross into France (Annemasse, Saint-Julien-en-Genevois) on weekends for groceries — saving 30–40% on food and household products. Works best for C Permit holders or those with a French border crossing card.

Transport

Geneva has excellent public transport — trams, buses, and lake boats run by TPG. A monthly pass costs CHF 70–85. For those commuting from outside the city, the GA national pass costs CHF 3,995/year (2nd class) — used by many who live in cheaper cantons and work in Geneva.

A car in the city is rarely necessary and expensive to maintain: insurance CHF 1,200–2,000/year, fuel ~CHF 1.80/litre, city parking CHF 3–6/hour.

What Changes With Children

Children change the financial equation completely in Geneva.

Public school: Free and good quality. Teaching in French. For children arriving without French, there are integration classes.

International school: CHF 25,000 to CHF 45,000 per child per year. For families who want children to maintain their home education system, this is often the only option. On a CHF 100,000 salary, one international school consumes 25–45% of gross income in tuition alone.

Nursery/crèche: CHF 2,000 to CHF 3,000/month per child full-time. Geneva's cantonal subsidies can significantly reduce this depending on income.

What's Left to Save and Invest

With CHF 100,000 gross, a single person without children can save CHF 2,000–2,500/month — before contributing to Pillar 3a.

Contributing the maximum to Pillar 3a (CHF 7,258/year = ~CHF 605/month) saves approximately CHF 2,400 in tax in Geneva. In practice, saving CHF 605 and receiving CHF 2,400 back in tax is the best return available in Switzerland.

Before You Ask

Is CHF 80,000 enough to live comfortably in Geneva?▾
Yes — for a single person without children who is willing to live in the suburbs and cook at home. The budget is tight but workable, with around CHF 2,000/month left to save. With children or higher lifestyle expectations, it becomes genuinely difficult.
Can I reduce my health insurance cost?▾
Yes. Choosing a higher annual deductible (CHF 1,500 or CHF 2,500 instead of the minimum CHF 300) reduces the monthly premium significantly. You can also choose an HMO or Telmed model where you see a GP or call a health line first — this also lowers premiums. Compare providers annually at priminfo.admin.ch.
Is it worth living across the border in France?▾
For some expats, yes — rent is 40–50% cheaper in the French border area (Annemasse, Gex, Ferney-Voltaire). But you pay French income tax on your Swiss salary under the cross-border worker agreement, and commuting adds time. It makes most sense for families who need space and cannot afford Geneva rents.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
When you want to optimise — Pillar 3a contributions, tax deductions, pension fund buy-ins. These decisions compound over time and are much easier to get right from the start than to correct years later.
