5 Tax Deductions Many Expats in Switzerland Overlook

Quick Answer: Many expats file their Swiss tax return every year and assume everything is in order. What fewer people ask is whether they are claiming every deduction available to them. Some of the most valuable deductions in the Swiss system are entirely legal, well-established — and consistently missed by international residents who were simply never told they existed.

Key Takeaways

  • Pillar 3a contribution limit for 2026: CHF 7,258 for employees — fully deductible from taxable income
  • Pension fund buy-ins can create significant one-year deductions with no upper limit in principle
  • In Geneva, families can deduct CHF 6,800 per child plus up to CHF 25,000 in childcare costs
  • Professional expense deductions are not always applied automatically — they often need to be claimed
  • From 2026, missed Pillar 3a contributions from 2025 onward can be retroactively recovered

Deduction #1: Pillar 3a Contributions

This is the most widely available tax deduction in Switzerland — and one of the most underused among expats. The Pillar 3a cap for 2026 is CHF 7,258 for employees, or 20% of net earned income up to CHF 36,288 for the self-employed. In Geneva, contributing the full CHF 7,258 saves approximately CHF 2,418 in tax that year.

🆕 New from 2026: Swiss law now allows retroactive contributions to Pillar 3a for years when you did not pay the full maximum — but only for gaps from 2025 onward. Gaps from 2024 and earlier cannot be filled.

Deduction #2: Voluntary Pension Fund Buy-Ins

If you arrived mid-career, you likely have a buy-in gap in your Pillar 2 fund. You can fill that gap voluntarily, and every franc contributed is fully deductible. Example: you contribute CHF 20,000 into your pension fund this year — your taxable income drops by CHF 20,000. At Geneva marginal rates, that saves approximately CHF 6,000 to CHF 8,000 in tax in a single year.

⚠ After a buy-in contribution, there is a three-year waiting period before you can withdraw that money as a lump sum. Timing matters — review this with an advisor before proceeding.

Deduction #3: Professional Expenses

Many employees assume work-related expenses are handled automatically. Often they are not. The professional expenses flat rate is 3% of net salary (minimum CHF 2,000, maximum CHF 4,000 federal). Also deductible: commuting costs (capped at CHF 3,300), meals away from home, home office costs, work equipment, and work-related training.

Deduction #4: Childcare Expenses

In Geneva, two separate child-related deductions exist: a child deduction of CHF 6,800 per child per year applied automatically, and a third-party childcare deduction of up to CHF 25,000 per child per year for documented costs paid to nurseries and childminders. The second is the one most commonly missed.

Deduction #5: Training and Continuing Education

Professional development costs are deductible when they relate to your current field of work. This includes courses, certifications, professional memberships, and in some cases language training relevant to your role. The deduction is often missed because many expats assume it only applies to formal academic programmes.

Expat A — CHF 150,000 salary

No Pillar 3a. No buy-in. No professional expenses claimed.

Pays tax on the full CHF 150,000.
Expat B — CHF 150,000 salary

Pillar 3a: CHF 7,258. Buy-in: CHF 20,000. Professional expenses: CHF 3,000.

Pays tax on approximately CHF 119,742 — saving CHF 8,000–12,000 in tax using entirely legal deductions.

Before You Ask

Do these deductions apply to B Permit holders?▾
Some do, depending on income level and circumstances. Pillar 3a is available to most employees with Swiss earned income. Pension buy-ins and some professional expense deductions typically become more relevant after obtaining a C Permit and filing a full tax return.
How much can Pillar 3a actually save?▾
In Geneva, at a typical professional salary, the full CHF 7,258 contribution saves approximately CHF 2,418 per year. Over ten years, that is more than CHF 24,000 in tax savings from a single deduction.
Is a pension fund buy-in always worth it?▾
Not always. The three-year blocking period and your specific pension fund situation need to be considered. A review with an advisor is strongly recommended before proceeding.
Can I open a Pillar 3a account as a foreign national?▾
Yes. Any person living and working in Switzerland with AHV-liable income can open a Pillar 3a account and claim the deduction — regardless of nationality or permit type.
