FBAR and FATCA Explained: What Americans in Switzerland Must File Every Year

Quick Answer: Most Americans in Switzerland know they need to file a US tax return every year. Fewer know that two separate reporting obligations — FBAR and FATCA — exist entirely apart from the tax return itself. You can owe zero US tax and still face tens of thousands of dollars in penalties for missing these filings.

Key Takeaways

  • FBAR applies if your foreign accounts exceed $10,000 combined at any point during the year
  • FATCA Form 8938 applies to higher thresholds — starting at $200,000 at year-end for single filers living abroad
  • FBAR and FATCA are separate filings with different deadlines and different agencies
  • You can owe zero US tax and still face tens of thousands of dollars in FBAR penalties
  • If you have never filed FBAR, catch-up options exist — but acting before the IRS contacts you means zero penalties

What Is FBAR?

FBAR stands for Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts — officially FinCEN Form 114. You must file if the total of all your foreign account balances combined exceeds $10,000 at any point during the year. The threshold applies to the total of all accounts combined, not to each account individually. This means two accounts of $6,000 each trigger the filing — even though neither individually exceeds the threshold. FBAR covers bank accounts, foreign brokerage accounts, insurance or annuity plans with cash value, and retirement accounts.

What Is FATCA Form 8938?

FATCA requires a separate form filed directly with the IRS as part of your annual tax return. For Americans living outside the United States: single filers — file if total foreign financial assets exceeded $200,000 at year-end or $300,000 at any point. Married filing jointly — file if total exceeded $400,000 at year-end or $600,000 at any point. Filing one does not satisfy the other — both may need to be filed.

The Penalties Are Severe

FBAR non-wilful violation: up to $16,536 per annual report. Wilful violation: the greater of $165,353 or 50% of the account balance — assessed per account, per year. Criminal penalties can reach $250,000 and five years imprisonment. Your Swiss bank reports to the IRS that your account had a maximum balance of CHF 150,000 in 2025. Your FBAR reports only CHF 80,000. This mismatch automatically triggers a review.

Two Americans, Two Different Situations

American A — 3 years in Switzerland

Swiss bank account CHF 45,000 + brokerage CHF 30,000. Files US tax return every year but has never filed an FBAR.

Three years of missed filings. The Streamlined Procedures may allow catch-up without penalties — but only if the IRS has not already initiated contact.
American B — 8 years in Switzerland

Swiss bank account + pension fund + investment portfolio totalling CHF 380,000. Has never filed FBAR or Form 8938.

Both obligations likely unmet. Eight years of missed filings. Professional advice is essential before attempting to catch up independently. Which situation sounds closer to yours?

What If You Have Never Filed FBAR?

Before the IRS contacts you — zero penalties: If you have properly reported all income from foreign accounts on your US tax returns, the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures allow you to file back FBARs with no penalty. The Streamlined Foreign Offshore Program waives all penalties for eligible taxpayers who come forward voluntarily.

After the IRS contacts you — full penalties apply. Once the IRS initiates contact, the voluntary programs are no longer available. Acting proactively is always the better and less expensive option.

Before You Ask

Do I need to file FBAR if I owe no US tax?▾
Yes. FBAR is a reporting requirement — not a tax. It exists regardless of whether any US tax is owed. Many expats mistakenly believe that paying foreign taxes removes reporting obligations. It does not.
Does my Swiss pension fund count for FBAR?▾
FBAR covers retirement accounts with some exceptions. Whether your specific Swiss pension fund triggers the FBAR depends on its structure. Professional advice is recommended to confirm.
Can the IRS already see my Swiss accounts?▾
In most cases, yes. Swiss banks report account information to the Swiss Federal Tax Administration under FATCA agreements, which then shares it with the IRS. Assuming non-disclosure goes unnoticed is increasingly unrealistic.
What if my accounts are jointly held with a non-US spouse?▾
Joint accounts are still reportable. The FBAR threshold applies to your share of the account. If you have signature authority over accounts you do not own, those may also need to be reported.
