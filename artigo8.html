FBAR and FATCA Explained: What Americans in Switzerland Must Report Every Year

Quick Answer: Yes — on top of your tax return, almost every American in Switzerland has to file a list of their foreign accounts every year (the FBAR), and those with more assets a second one (Form 8938, under FATCA). They are not taxes: they are reports of what you hold. Forgetting them can cost thousands of dollars in penalties, even with not a cent of tax owed.

What Is the FBAR?

In plain terms: an annual list of your accounts outside the US, sent to FinCEN — a department of the US Treasury that fights financial crime. Its official name is FinCEN Form 114.

It is required when the combined total of all your accounts outside the US goes above $10,000 at any point in the year. It is the total that counts, not each account: two accounts of $6,000 make $12,000 — and already require an FBAR, even though neither crosses the limit on its own. With a Swiss current account and savings account, the limit is crossed almost every year.

What Are FATCA and Form 8938?

In plain terms: FATCA is a US law that requires banks worldwide — Swiss banks included — to report their American clients' accounts to the IRS (the US tax authority). On your side, the law created Form 8938: a list of your financial assets abroad, attached to your tax return.

The Penalties Are Severe

The law distinguishes between people who didn't know and people who hid on purpose:

Non-wilful
$0
per missing FBAR — per year, not per account. Forgetting or not knowing.
Wilful
$0
or 50% of the balance, whichever is greater — per account, per year. Knew and didn't report.
Form 8938 missing
$0
plus up to $50,000 more if you still don't file after the IRS notifies you

In the most serious cases there is criminal prosecution: up to $250,000 and five years in prison.

And don't count on going unnoticed: your Swiss bank reports balances to the IRS under FATCA. An account that shows up on the bank's side but not on your FBAR is easy to spot.

What If You Have Never Filed an FBAR?

You can always catch up. What changes everything is the timing.

Before the IRS contacts you
No penalties — if you qualify

If you live outside the US (at least 330 days abroad in one of the last three years) and the failure was not intentional, you can use the Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures: file the last three years of tax returns and the last six years of FBARs, and the IRS applies no penalties.

The voluntary programs close, the penalties described above apply, and the process becomes a formal audit.

Note: until June 2026 there was an even simpler route for people who had only forgotten the FBAR. The IRS removed it from its website in July 2026, without explanation. That makes choosing the right path today even more worth a conversation with an advisor.

Common Mistakes

1

Thinking a Swiss account isn't "foreign" because you live in Switzerland

For the US, any account outside the US is foreign — including the account where your salary is paid in Geneva.

2

Reporting only half of a joint account

For a joint account — even with a spouse who isn't American — you report the full value of the account, not your half.

✕ Wrong
50% — your half
✓ Right
100% — the full balance
3

Forgetting accounts where you only have signing authority

If you can move money in an account that isn't yours — for example, your employer's company account — it may also need to go on your FBAR.

Before You Ask

Does my Swiss pension fund count for the FBAR?▾
It can. Retirement accounts are included in the FBAR with some exceptions, and it depends on how your fund is structured (Pillar 2 or Pillar 3a). It is worth confirming with an advisor.
Can the IRS already see my Swiss accounts?▾
In most cases, yes. Today Swiss banks report their American clients' accounts to the IRS under FATCA. Under the new agreement between Switzerland and the US, expected in 2027, this exchange becomes automatic, through the Swiss Federal Tax Administration.
When should I speak with an advisor?▾
As soon as possible — especially if you have missing FBARs, if your assets are above the Form 8938 thresholds, or if you are not sure everything has been reported.

FBAR and FATCA are among the areas where Americans in Switzerland most often discover a problem — not because they wanted to hide anything, but because they didn't know these obligations existed. The good news is that there is still a way to catch up without penalties. The bad news is that it disappears once the IRS contacts you.
